Biomimetic Solar

An artistic and technological investigation into solar panels made through biomimetic design, organic material experimentation and community-oriented DIY production.

Project Abstract

The project rethinks the solar panel as a functional device, an art object, and a pedagogical tool. Drawing from plant morphology and natural growth processes, it explores how solar technologies can be reimagined through alternative forms of design and production.

By applying electrochemical coatings to diverse surfaces, the work expands from screen-printing techniques to architectural-scale mural interventions. It focuses on reproducible fabrication methods, enabling photovoltaic structures to emerge from accessible, low-cost processes rooted in DIY culture and collective experimentation.

Physarum

Conceptual basis
Plant morphology

Plant morphology provides the conceptual foundation of the project. Rather than treating plants as visual references, the research focuses on their structural intelligence: how they capture light, distribute energy and adapt to changing environmental conditions.

Leaf venation, branching systems and surface organisation are understood as optimised solutions developed through evolution. These morphologies are not arbitrary; they emerge from the need to maximise efficiency with minimal material.

Through biomimetics, these principles are translated into the design of solar systems. Instead of relying on flat, standardised panels, the project explores alternative geometries that could improve energy distribution, light capture and spatial integration.

At the same time, this approach questions the sterile and purely industrial construction of conventional solar technologies, proposing a shift toward systems that are more integrated, adaptive and materially expressive.

Generative logic
Physarum / Turing

The project also draws from generative systems such as reaction-diffusion models, first proposed by Alan Turing in 1952, and later developed through specific formulations such as the Gray–Scott model.

Turing’s work explains how many patterns found in living organisms — such as the stripes of zebras, the spots of leopards or the markings of fish — can emerge from simple chemical interactions. Through the process of reaction and diffusion, these systems generate organised structures that distribute themselves efficiently across a surface.

In the Gray–Scott model, the interaction between two virtual chemicals produces a wide range of patterns — from spots and stripes to branching and coral-like formations — offering a computational framework to explore these natural processes.

Physarum polycephalum, a slime mould studied by researchers such as Toshiyuki Nakagaki, offers a biological counterpart. It forms adaptive networks that optimise paths between resources, continuously reorganising itself in response to environmental conditions.

Together, these systems provide a framework for understanding form as an emergent process. Rather than designing fixed geometries, the project explores how solar structures could be generated through rules of growth, distribution and efficiency, similar to those found in living systems.

01 / Project Frame

From solar technology to cultural artifact

The project starts from a critical view of the green transition when it reproduces technocratic and extractivist logics. Rather than treating the solar panel as a neutral industrial unit, this research proposes it as a symbolic, aesthetic and civic interface.

Biomimicry provides the formal intelligence of the project, not as decorative imitation but as a study of how vegetal systems optimise light, distribute matter and adapt to environmental conditions. It frames environmental questions through relations between social practice, material experimentation and collective imaginaries. The project opens a dialogue between plant structures, mineral processes and technical fabrication.

02 / Project Principles
Biomimetic design

Study leaf venation, spiral arrangements, radial organisations and adaptive geometries that maximise light exposure and structural efficiency.

Material experimentation

Test translucent cellulose, conductive inks, copper plates, oxides, organic pigments and biodegradable supports in a low-tech research environment.

Distributed pedagogy

Turn each prototype into a transmissible tool: something that can be shown, narrated, replicated and shared through workshops and open documentation.

Solar justice

Shift renewable energy from a centralised commodity toward a collectively owned practice rooted in accessibility, autonomy and local making.

02b / Morphological Translation

From leaf venation to silkscreen pattern

The formal logic of plant venation — midrib, secondary veins, tertiary network — is translated into a geometric pattern suitable for screen printing. Turing reaction-diffusion simulation generates the intermediate abstraction.

03 / Development

Research to installation

Phase 1

Research & Design

Study plant morphologies, assess translucent photovoltaic materials and produce initial sketches and digital prototypes.

Phase 2

Prototype Development

Screen print first modules, test different leaf-based patterns and evaluate basic efficiency under varying light conditions.

Phase 3

Installation & Testing

Design an immersive display, refine positioning for natural and artificial light, and document all adjustments.

Phase 4

Exhibition & Dissemination

Present prototypes publicly through exhibition, workshops and open educational materials that enable further experimentation.

04 / Impact

A regenerative model of artistic technology

Artistic Innovation Recasts renewable energy infrastructure as a site for visual culture, material experimentation and public engagement.

Community Inclusion Uses workshops, collaborative making and accessible formats to involve low-income and disabled communities in solar knowledge production.

Environmental Responsibility Prioritises local and biodegradable material directions, modular construction and a circular approach to fabrication and maintenance.
